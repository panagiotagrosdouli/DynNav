\begin{abstract}
A robot can reach the same geometric location through different executed paths while retaining different future return options when its actions activate topology hazards. We study this setting through action-triggered stochastic closures: traversing selected directed transitions activates future closure events that may disconnect the robot from a designated safe set. We formulate safe-return reliability on the augmented state consisting of robot position and activated hazards, and show that if two histories reaching the same position have return reliabilities $R_1$ and $R_2$, any deterministic endpoint-only estimator incurs worst-history absolute error at least $|R_1-R_2|/2$. We implement an exact small-hazard return-connectivity oracle and a history-conditioned A* planner, and compare against shortest-path, state-only marginal-risk, hard safe-return, and structural approximation baselines. Controlled, frozen heterogeneous, and three predeclared geometric evaluations show the representation effect under the tested model: the state-only marginal baseline aliases histories and reproduces the risky shortest-path outcomes, while history-conditioned planning avoids activated hazards in these settings. A hard safe-return constraint can match this behavior, and a critical-cut approximation fails on a multi-cell cutset. The evidence therefore supports a narrow representation claim---executed hazard-activation history can be necessary for estimating future return-connectivity---rather than universal superiority of a particular objective or planner.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Planning for collision avoidance or low expected traversal risk is not equivalent to preserving the ability to recover. A robot may enter a corridor safely under the current map and still make a commitment that leaves no return route after a later closure. Returnability has long appeared in safe exploration \cite{moldovan2012safe}, and later work formalizes safe-return policies, online uncertain-MDP planning with return constraints, contingency feasibility, backup-plan safety, and predictive commitment \cite{zhang2022online,guo2023safereturn,stephens2024safeexploration,jung2025contingency,srirangam2026scramppi,tao2024backup,han2026rcsp}. These results rule out any broad novelty claim for ``planning while keeping a way back.''

History-dependent robot risk is also established prior art. Xiao, Dufek, and Murphy formalize locale-, action-, and traverse-dependent motion risk and develop a directional planner that retains history needed to evaluate non-Markovian risk \cite{xiao2020risk}. Therefore, neither history-dependent planning nor probabilistic history-dependent risk is new in itself.

This paper asks a narrower question: \emph{what if the robot's own path history changes which future environmental topology hazards are active?} Consider two trajectories that end at the same geometric cell. One traversed a transition that causes a door, gate, or passage behind the robot to become likely to close; the other avoided that transition. If the closure process is action-triggered, the two trajectories have different future return-connectivity despite sharing the same current position and static map. The relevant planning state is therefore not position alone.

Decision-dependent uncertainty is a mature topic beyond robotics \cite{doan2022endogenous}, and robot planning has considered utilization-driven component degradation \cite{baldes2024fallible}, dynamic door-closing workspaces \cite{liu2021door}, and nondeterministic environmental events \cite{perret1996events}. Our contribution is not the generic idea that actions influence future uncertainty. Instead, we isolate and experimentally test the information lost when an action-triggered stochastic process changes the \emph{environment's future return-connectivity} but a planner retains only state-only marginal closure probabilities.

Stochastically changing navigation topology has also been studied in AFADA, where an active modular environment manages topology and distributes routing instructions to robots \cite{kameyama2021active}. DynNav shares the setting of stochastic topology change but uses a different causal model: selected transitions executed by the robot activate future closure hazards, and the evaluation quantity is return-connectivity conditioned on those activations. The augmented state is a conventional finite-MDP construction; the paper does not claim novelty for memory, history augmentation, or Markovization. The state-only marginal planner below is therefore interpreted as a deliberately restricted representation ablation, not as a proxy for all MDP/POMDP methods.

Traversal-dependent topology is itself established. Carmesin \emph{et al.} formalize self-deleting graphs in which visiting vertices can delete future edges \cite{carmesin2023selfdeleting}; Dvo\v{r}\'ak \emph{et al.} study shortest-path finding directly on such traversal-dependent graphs \cite{dvorak2025selfdeleting}; and Frenkel \emph{et al.} apply the model to coverage with self-induced obstacles, where the robot's own traversal changes future feasible successors \cite{frenkel2026selfinduced}. We therefore do not claim novelty for action-induced topology change, self-deleting graph structure, path-history-dependent traversability, or history-sensitive graph search. Those works use deterministic deletion semantics. The model here instead separates an executed trigger from a stochastic future closure realization and evaluates the probability that the resulting environment remains connected to a designated safe set. Our empirical question is correspondingly narrower: what safe-return-reliability information is lost when activated stochastic hazards are marginalized away from the planning state?

The contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item an explicit action-triggered stochastic topology model in which executed transitions activate future closure events and safe-return reliability is conditioned on the resulting activation state;
  \item a same-position/different-history construction together with a representation lower bound: collapsing two histories with reliabilities $R_1$ and $R_2$ to one deterministic endpoint-only estimate incurs worst-history error at least $|R_1-R_2|/2$;
  \item an exact small-hazard return-connectivity oracle and augmented-state A* reference planner, evaluated against shortest-path, state-only marginal-risk, and hard safe-return baselines;
  \item frozen paired evaluations across probability/horizon transfer and three hand-authored geometries, plus an objective-sensitivity study that explicitly retains cases where hard safe-return constraints match the history-conditioned route;
  \item a structural critical-cut approximation with both a measured scaling regime and an adversarial multi-cell-cut counterexample that exposes its failure boundary.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\subsection{Safe return and contingency feasibility}
Moldovan and Abbeel make returnability central to safe exploration in non-ergodic MDPs \cite{moldovan2012safe}. Zhang and Guo enforce high-probability return to home states while learning an uncertain MDP online \cite{zhang2022online}, and Guo \emph{et al.} jointly synthesize outbound and safe-return behavior under probabilistic temporal tasks \cite{guo2023safereturn}. Safe exploration methods also evaluate multi-step reachability and returnability probabilities before visiting uncertain regions \cite{stephens2024safeexploration}. Backup Plan Constrained MPC optimizes primary and alternative mission feasibility \cite{tao2024backup}. Contingency-MPPI embeds contingency generation inside nominal planning, and SCRAMPPI uses Hamilton--Jacobi reachability to certify that a safe-set trajectory exists from every state on the nominal plan \cite{jung2025contingency,srirangam2026scramppi}. These methods are broader and, in several cases, provide stronger safety or dynamics guarantees than our grid abstraction. Our distinction is not the safe-return objective itself, but the representation of future return-connectivity when the set of active environmental hazards is caused by executed robot transitions.

\subsection{History-dependent motion risk}
Xiao \emph{et al.} explicitly reject a purely state-dependent risk model, classify action- and traverse-dependent risks, and plan with directional history information \cite{xiao2020risk}. Their framework is close to ours in the reason memory is required: two arrivals at a geometric location need not have the same path risk. Our problem variable differs. We condition on a set of action-triggered \emph{future environmental closures} and evaluate the induced probability that the current state remains connected to a safe set. Thus the object being propagated is future return-connectivity under a changed environmental hazard state, rather than accumulated locomotion risk alone. We treat the 2020 result as a direct novelty constraint, not as a weak baseline.

\subsection{Traversal-dependent topology}
Self-deleting graphs explicitly model traversal-dependent structure: processing a vertex can delete edges from the residual graph \cite{carmesin2023selfdeleting}. Shortest pathfinding on this model has been studied algorithmically and shown to be computationally hard in several restricted graph classes \cite{dvorak2025selfdeleting}. In robotics, Frenkel \emph{et al.} use self-deleting graphs to model coverage with self-induced obstacles \cite{frenkel2026selfinduced}. These works are direct novelty constraints because they already establish that the robot's previous traversal can alter future graph feasibility. DynNav differs in the modeled random variable and evaluation target: executing a trigger activates, but does not immediately realize, a stochastic future closure; the quantity of interest is the conditional probability that a safe set remains reachable. We therefore treat deterministic self-deletion as neighboring prior art rather than claiming the general history--topology connection as new.

\subsection{Predictive commitment and dynamic topology}
RCSP studies commands that are locally safe but commit a robot to a bottleneck that moving obstacles may soon close \cite{han2026rcsp}. Dynamic door-closing work has used POMDP belief-tree reasoning for self-protective manipulation behavior \cite{liu2021door}, while nondeterministic planning has long represented exogenous events and robust plans \cite{perret1996events}. These works motivate proactive reasoning about future changes; they do not by themselves establish that our action-triggered return-topology abstraction is novel.

\subsection{Endogenous uncertainty and degradation}
Optimization under endogenous uncertainty explicitly models probability distributions that depend on decisions \cite{doan2022endogenous}. Baldes \emph{et al.} model utilization-driven, state-action-dependent actuator failures and their long-term effect on future control capability \cite{baldes2024fallible}. Consequently, neither ``history matters'' nor ``actions can reduce future capabilities'' is a contribution here. We instead focus on a falsifiable robotics question: whether history contains return-connectivity information that is discarded when environmental closure probabilities are represented only as state-only marginals.

\section{Problem Formulation}
Let $G=(V,E)$ be a traversable grid graph, $x_t\in V$ the robot position, and $\Ssafe\subseteq V$ a safe set. A topology hazard
\begin{equation}
h_i=(e_i,c_i,p_i)
\end{equation}
contains a directed trigger transition $e_i\in E$, a currently traversable closure cell $c_i\in V$, and a future closure probability $p_i\in[0,1]$. Traversing $e_i$ activates hazard $i$. For path history $H_t=(x_0,\ldots,x_t)$, define
\begin{equation}
\Aset(H_t)=\{i\mid e_i\text{ occurs in }H_t\}.
\end{equation}
The augmented planning state is $z_t=(x_t,\Aset(H_t))$. Figure~\ref{fig:history-gap} illustrates the minimal same-endpoint distinction.

For a fixed augmented state, each distinct active closure cell denotes one latent Bernoulli closure event with a fixed probability, and these events are independent across cells. Multiple triggers mapped to the same cell activate that same event; they do not create independent repeated trials. The model has no within-mission closure realization or observation update: the future closure sample is drawn for the return-connectivity evaluation, and the robot cell is conditioned traversable at that instant. Under these assumptions, the active trigger set determines the active-cell probability map, so $(x,\Aset)$ is a sufficient Markov state for the modeled return query. It need not be minimal: trigger identifiers that induce identical active-cell maps can be merged. Correlated closures, changing probabilities, partial observations of realized events, or time-dependent closures require additional state (for example, a joint belief or event-time state), and are outside this model. Let $C$ denote the random set of realized closures. We define exact safe-return reliability
\begin{equation}
\Rret(x,\Aset)=
\Pr\!\left[\exists\text{ path in }G\setminus C\text{ from }x\text{ to }\Ssafe\right].
\label{eq:return}
\end{equation}
For small active sets, Eq.~\eqref{eq:return} is computed exactly by enumerating closure realizations and testing connectivity.

\subsection{Why geometric state alone is insufficient}
A state-only marginal model maps position $x$ and a fixed marginal hazard field to a single return estimate $\widetilde R(x)$. It therefore cannot assign distinct values to two histories that end at the same $x$.

\textbf{Proposition 1 (history information gap).}
There exists a grid, safe set, trigger hazard, and histories $H^a,H^b$ with the same endpoint $x$ such that
\begin{equation}
\Rret(x,\Aset(H^a))\neq\Rret(x,\Aset(H^b)),
\end{equation}
while any deterministic state-only marginal model returns the same $\widetilde R(x)$ for both histories.

\emph{Proof.} Let one history traverse a trigger that activates closure of the unique bridge to the safe set with probability $p>0$, and let the other history reach the same endpoint without traversing that trigger. Then the triggered history has return reliability $1-p$ and the non-triggered history has reliability $1$. Since both end at the same $x$, a state-only function of $x$ and fixed marginals must return one common value. \hfill$\square$

\section{Planning Methods}
\subsection{Exact history-aware A*}
Search is performed over augmented states $(x,\Aset)$. For transition $z\rightarrow z'$, the soft-history objective uses
\begin{equation}
c(z,z')=1+\lambda\left(1-\Rret(z')\right),
\label{eq:cost}
\end{equation}
with nonnegative recoverability weight $\lambda$. The online return-oracle calls used to select the route are included in measured planning latency; post-hoc diagnostics are excluded.

\subsection{State-only probability-aware baseline}
The state-only baseline receives the same closure cells and marginal probabilities $p_i$ but drops trigger history. It plans using return-path reliability under this fixed marginal field. Thus it is not an uninformed shortest-path baseline: it knows where future closures may occur and with what marginal probability, but it cannot know whether a particular path actually activates them.

\subsection{Hard safe-return baseline}
A strong constraint baseline accepts a successor only when
\begin{equation}
\Rret(z')\geq\tau,
\end{equation}
for a threshold $\tau$. This abstracts safe-return and contingency methods that enforce backup feasibility rather than optimize a soft penalty.

\subsection{Critical-cut approximation}
Exact enumeration scales exponentially in the number of active hazards. We therefore evaluate a structural approximation that removes each active hazard cell individually and identifies those whose single removal disconnects the robot from $\Ssafe$. Multiplying the survival probabilities of these individually critical cells gives
\begin{equation}
R_{\mathrm{cut}}=\prod_{i\in K}(1-p_i).
\end{equation}
Because multi-cell cutsets may disconnect the graph even when no individual cell is critical, $R_{\mathrm{cut}}$ is an upper bound in general. We treat this approximation as scalability infrastructure, not as a novel graph-theoretic contribution.

\section{Experimental Design}
All stochastic comparisons use common random numbers: a seed generates one latent draw for every declared closure event, and a planner realizes only the events it activated. This preserves paired event-index outcomes even when planners activate different subsets. Irreversible failure is operationalized as post-closure inability to reach the safe set in the resulting grid. Failed trials remain in binary denominators; undefined continuous metrics are counted explicitly rather than silently removed.

We evaluate eight complementary questions.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{Information gap:} can identical geometric endpoints have different exact return reliability solely because of history?
  \item \emph{Analytic route switching:} does the planner follow a predicted length--fragility boundary rather than a post-hoc tuned pattern?
  \item \emph{Stochastic execution:} does history conditioning change irreversible-failure outcomes under sampled future closures?
  \item \emph{State-only falsification:} does a probability-aware planner with the same marginals reproduce the history-aware behavior?
  \item \emph{Parameter/horizon transfer:} does the result survive frozen heterogeneous probabilities and unseen module counts?
  \item \emph{Geometric transfer:} does the state-only/history distinction survive three predeclared hand-built topologies outside the repeated-module geometry?
  \item \emph{Objective sensitivity:} how does the soft history objective compare with hard safe-return constraints across a predeclared parameter grid?
  \item \emph{Approximation/scaling boundary:} what does the critical-cut approximation save, and where does it become optimistic?
\end{enumerate}

The development sweeps use homogeneous closure probabilities. Before inspecting outcomes, a separate repeated-module evaluation family was frozen with heterogeneous per-trigger probabilities and unseen module counts: six modules with probabilities $(.15,.25,.35,.55,.75,.85)$, seven modules with $(.80,.20,.70,.30,.60,.40,.90)$, and eight modules with $(.10,.90,.20,.80,.35,.65,.45,.75)$. Each scenario uses 500 paired execution seeds. This tests probability-pattern and horizon transfer within the same procedural topology family.

A second held-out family was frozen with three different hand-built grid topologies: a fork, an L-shaped room, and a two-trigger chamber. Starts, goals, safe sets, trigger transitions, closure cells, and probabilities were fixed before the 500-seed outcomes were inspected. After those worlds were frozen, a separate objective-sensitivity protocol was committed before its outcomes were inspected: soft weights $\lambda\in\{0,1,2,4,8,16\}$ and hard thresholds $\tau\in\{.50,.70,.80,.90,.95,.99\}$, again with 500 common-random-number seeds per configuration. Planning infeasibility is reported separately from post-closure irreversible failure.

\section{Results}
\subsection{Same state, different history}
The counterfactual benchmark sweeps $p\in\{0.1,0.3,0.5,0.7,0.9\}$. The safe history has exact return reliability $1$, while the trigger-taking history has reliability $1-p$. A state-only marginal model assigns $1-p$ to both because it cannot condition on whether the trigger was traversed. Across the five cases, the mean history separation is $0.5$ and the maximum is $0.9$; the triggered-history invariant is matched with zero numerical error.

\subsection{Analytic phase boundary}
In a controlled world with a direct path of length $4$ and a trigger-avoiding detour of length $4+2d$, exactly one direct transition incurs fragility $\lambda p$. The predicted route switch is therefore
\begin{equation}
\lambda p>2d.
\end{equation}
Across 75 combinations of detour depth, closure probability, and weight, the exact history-aware planner matches the analytic route choice in all 75 cases. The shortest-path baseline never detours.

The shortest and state-only probability-aware planners select the same trigger-taking path and consequently have identical binary outcomes. The exact-history, critical-cut, and hard $\tau=0.9$ planners avoid all triggers for the high recoverability weight used in this controlled experiment.

\subsection{Soft versus hard objective sensitivity}
The predeclared parameter sweep provides an important negative scope result. In the fork, soft $\lambda=0$ and $1$ select the risky length-6 route with failure rate 0.810, while $\lambda\ge2$ selects the length-8 zero-hazard route with zero failure. In the L-room and chamber, $\lambda=0$ retains the risky route, while every tested $\lambda\ge1$ selects a zero-hazard route. By contrast, every frozen hard threshold $\tau\in\{.50,.70,.80,.90,.95,.99\}$ selects a zero-hazard, zero-failure route in all three worlds. All tested configurations are planner-feasible.

This result prevents an objective-level overclaim. The geometric held-out experiments support the value of representing activated hazard history; they do not show that the soft cumulative penalty dominates a hard safe-return constraint. On these worlds the hard baseline is at least as effective and has lower retained single-run planning latency. The two objectives answer different operating questions, and neither is universally preferred by the present evidence.

These are representative wall-clock values from one retained CI run rather than a statistically characterized performance distribution, and the zero cut error applies only to this controlled series-critical family.

\subsection{Joint-cut adversarial case}
The critical-cut approximation is exact on the series family because each relevant closure cell is individually critical. We therefore construct a different topology with two parallel return corridors. Neither closure cell alone disconnects the safe set, but closing both does. The lattice has two equal-length goal routes (length 6), so route choice isolates the recoverability estimate rather than a distance tradeoff.

Across all nine combinations $p\in\{0.2,0.5,0.8\}$ and $\lambda\in\{2,4,8\}$, exact history-aware planning selects the route that activates no hazards and retains exact return reliability 1. The cut planner selects an equal-length route that activates both hazards in all nine cases. Because neither cell is individually critical, it reports $R_{\mathrm{cut}}=1$. Exact post-hoc evaluation of that same route is instead $1-p^2$: 0.96, 0.75, and 0.36 for the three probabilities. Thus the artifact contains nine of nine optimistic cut estimates and a route-history disagreement rate of 1.0. This is an explicit counterexample to using individually-critical cuts as a general return-reliability surrogate.

\section{Discussion}
The experiments support a narrow claim. When actions activate future topology hazards, path history can contain information about safe-return connectivity that is absent from a state-only marginal hazard map. This is not equivalent to claiming that history-aware planning is universally safer, that soft recoverability costs dominate hard contingency constraints, or that the proposed model subsumes general dynamic navigation.

The state-only null result is more informative than comparison only against shortest path. Both planners know the same closure locations and marginal probabilities. Their divergence arises because one conditions on the executed trigger set and the other does not. This distinction survives both the frozen heterogeneous repeated-module family and three separately frozen hand-built topologies. The latter broaden the evidence beyond one procedural geometry, but three constructed grids remain far from arbitrary-map or physical-environment generalization.

The hard safe-return results sharpen this interpretation. A fixed $\tau=0.9$ differs from the soft objective in the eight-module heterogeneous case, but the complete frozen threshold sweep on the three geometric worlds selects the same zero-hazard behavior for every tested threshold. Thus the strongest conclusion concerns the state representation---activated environmental hazard history---rather than superiority of a particular soft objective. The soft formulation remains useful when one wants an explicit path-length/fragility tradeoff, whereas hard constraints encode an admissibility requirement.

The exact oracle is useful for small controlled worlds and for validating approximations, but its exponential closure enumeration is not a scalable navigation solution. The critical-cut approximation removes most of this cost in series-articulation families. Its joint-cut failure is therefore an important design boundary: fast structural approximations require multi-cell cut reasoning, scenario sampling, or another estimator when redundant return paths can fail jointly.

The closest history-dependent risk work also narrows the interpretation. Xiao \emph{et al.} already establish that robot risk can depend on action and traversal history and that planning may need a history-sensitive state representation \cite{xiao2020risk}. Our evidence does not supersede that result. It isolates a different state variable: which future environmental topology changes have been activated and what that implies for safe-set connectivity.

\section{Threats to Validity}
The homogeneous and heterogeneous series families contain critical return bridges and were designed to expose the mechanism, so a trigger-avoiding policy has a structural advantage when closure probabilities are appreciable. The three geometric held-out worlds reduce dependence on that single repeated topology but are still hand-built to contain action-triggered return-critical closures. The state-only baseline is intentionally given the same marginal closure probabilities but an incorrect conditional model; its failure demonstrates information loss under that modeling choice, not weakness of every possible Markov representation. A sufficiently rich Markov state could include the activated-hazard set, which is exactly our augmented state.

Parameter selection remains a concern for any weighted objective. The principal soft-planner execution results use $\lambda=8$, but the predeclared geometric sweep spans $\lambda\in\{0,1,2,4,8,16\}$ and hard thresholds from 0.50 to 0.99. That sweep shows both where the soft route switches and where hard constraints match the zero-hazard behavior; it therefore reduces, but does not eliminate, parameter-selection concerns. Monte Carlo uncertainty remains in empirical failure rates; paired binary effects and exact McNemar tests are used because seeds are shared across planners.

\section{Limitations}
The current study uses discrete grid connectivity, known trigger locations, known closure probabilities, and mostly independent Bernoulli future closures. A scenario-based oracle demonstrates that equal marginals can be optimistic under correlated closures, but correlated uncertainty is not yet integrated into the online planner. The stochastic execution experiments sample topology events after commitment; they do not model continuous robot dynamics, perception delay, collision, or contact.

The repository contains a ROS~2 Jazzy/Nav2 C++ implementation of the history-conditioned planner and a frozen Gazebo action-trigger protocol. The C++ plugin retains activated hazards from externally observed executed transitions rather than from planned paths, and its search and plugin contracts are covered by software tests. No retained comparative action-triggered Gazebo outcome artifact is currently available, however. We therefore treat this as implementation and protocol evidence only and make no simulation-efficacy, hardware, collision-avoidance, kinodynamic-safety, or formal runtime-guarantee claim. The three geometric held-out worlds are evidence of mechanism replication across distinct hand-built topologies, not arbitrary-map or real-world geometric generalization.

Finally, literature search cannot prove novelty. Safe return, contingency planning, predictive commitment, history-dependent risk, action reversibility, endogenous uncertainty, dynamic doors, and utilization-driven degradation all have substantial prior art \cite{guo2023safereturn,xiao2020risk,jung2025contingency,srirangam2026scramppi,han2026rcsp,baldes2024fallible,liu2021door,doan2022endogenous}. The defensible contribution is the explicit action-triggered environmental return-topology formulation and its state-only information-gap evaluation, subject to continued prior-art review.

\section{Reproducibility}
The implementation records raw per-trial CSV files and machine-readable JSON summaries for every benchmark family. Focused continuous integration runs regression tests before executing the benchmark suite, then uploads all raw artifacts under the exact commit SHA. Planning-time instrumentation excludes post-hoc diagnostic profiling while retaining oracle work used online for route selection. Stochastic comparisons use fixed paired seeds and event-index common random numbers. The heterogeneous, geometric held-out, and soft-versus-hard parameter protocols were frozen before their respective outcomes were inspected. A machine-readable paper evidence manifest records the evidence-producing workflow runs, artifact identities and digests, metric values, and claim scope.

\section{Conclusion}
We studied navigation in which the robot's own transition history activates stochastic future closures that can destroy return connectivity. In this setting, the same geometric cell can correspond to different recoverability because the environment's future topology depends on how the robot arrived. Augmenting search with the activated hazard set captures this distinction; a state-only marginal-risk baseline cannot. The mechanism reproduces across repeated procedural families and three predeclared hand-built held-out topologies. A frozen objective sweep also shows that hard safe-return constraints can match the zero-hazard behavior, so the evidence supports the history-conditioned representation rather than universal superiority of the soft cost. A critical-cut approximation reduces computation in series-critical cases but fails on joint cutsets. These results establish a reproducible mechanism-level case for history-conditioned safe-return reasoning while clearly delimiting the additional evidence required for broader robotics claims.
