\begin{abstract}
A robot can reach the same geometric location through different executed paths while retaining different future return options when its actions activate topology hazards. We study action-triggered stochastic closures: selected executed transitions activate future closure events that may disconnect the robot from a designated safe set. Safe-return reliability is defined on the augmented state consisting of robot position and activated hazards. We give an elementary endpoint-aliasing bound and implement a history-conditioned A* planner with an exact small-hazard connectivity oracle. A primary state-only control uses the same exact oracle and additive objective but applies one fixed marginal hazard field, isolating activation history from estimator quality. In a frozen 96-scenario generated challenge suite where every start-to-goal route activates hazards, the history-conditioned planner is nonworse than the exact state-only control in all 96 scenarios and improves 36; mean post-closure return-infeasibility decreases from 0.744 to 0.666, with a scenario-level mean difference of $-0.078$ (95\% bootstrap CI $[-0.109,-0.050]$). Controlled and geometric tests, a hard safe-return comparison, and an adversarial cutset case further delimit the mechanism. The evidence supports a narrow representation claim---executed hazard-activation history can be necessary for estimating future return-connectivity---rather than universal superiority of a particular planner or objective.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Planning for collision avoidance or low expected traversal risk is not equivalent to preserving the ability to recover. A robot may enter a corridor safely under the current map and still make a commitment that leaves no return route after a later closure. Returnability has long appeared in safe exploration \cite{moldovan2012safe}, and later work formalizes safe-return policies, online uncertain-MDP planning with return constraints, contingency feasibility, backup-plan safety, and predictive commitment \cite{zhang2022online,guo2023safereturn,stephens2024safeexploration,jung2025contingency,srirangam2026scramppi,tao2024backup,han2026rcsp}. These results rule out any broad novelty claim for ``planning while keeping a way back.''

History-dependent robot risk is also established prior art. Xiao, Dufek, and Murphy formalize locale-, action-, and traverse-dependent motion risk and develop a directional planner that retains history needed to evaluate non-Markovian risk \cite{xiao2020risk}. Therefore, neither history-dependent planning nor probabilistic history-dependent risk is new in itself.

This paper asks a narrower question: \emph{what if the robot's own path history changes which future environmental topology hazards are active?} Consider two trajectories that end at the same geometric cell. One traversed a transition that causes a door, gate, or passage behind the robot to become likely to close; the other avoided that transition. If the closure process is action-triggered, the two trajectories have different future return-connectivity despite sharing the same current position and static map. The relevant planning state is therefore not position alone.

Decision-dependent uncertainty is a mature topic beyond robotics \cite{doan2022endogenous}, and robot planning has considered utilization-driven component degradation \cite{baldes2024fallible}, dynamic door-closing workspaces \cite{liu2021door}, and nondeterministic environmental events \cite{perret1996events}. Our contribution is not the generic idea that actions influence future uncertainty. Instead, we isolate and experimentally test the information lost when an action-triggered stochastic process changes the \emph{environment's future return-connectivity} but a planner retains only state-only marginal closure probabilities.

Stochastically changing navigation topology has also been studied in AFADA, where an active modular environment manages topology and distributes routing instructions to robots \cite{kameyama2021active}. DynNav shares the setting of stochastic topology change but uses a different causal model: selected transitions executed by the robot activate future closure hazards, and the evaluation quantity is return-connectivity conditioned on those activations. The augmented state is a conventional finite-MDP construction; the paper does not claim novelty for memory, history augmentation, or Markovization. The state-only fixed-field controls below are deliberately restricted representation ablations, not proxies for all MDP/POMDP methods.

Traversal-dependent topology is itself established. Carmesin \emph{et al.} formalize self-deleting graphs in which visiting vertices can delete future edges \cite{carmesin2023selfdeleting}; Dvo\v{r}\'ak \emph{et al.} study shortest-path finding directly on such traversal-dependent graphs \cite{dvorak2025selfdeleting}; and Frenkel \emph{et al.} apply the model to coverage with self-induced obstacles, where the robot's own traversal changes future feasible successors \cite{frenkel2026selfinduced}. We therefore do not claim novelty for action-induced topology change, self-deleting graph structure, path-history-dependent traversability, or history-sensitive graph search. Those works use deterministic deletion semantics. The model here instead separates an executed trigger from a stochastic future closure realization and evaluates the probability that the resulting environment remains connected to a designated safe set. Our empirical question is correspondingly narrower: what safe-return-reliability information is lost when activated stochastic hazards are marginalized away from the planning state?

The contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item an explicit action-triggered stochastic topology model in which executed transitions activate future closure events and safe-return reliability is conditioned on the resulting activation state;
  \item a same-position/different-history construction and an elementary endpoint-aliasing bound that quantifies information loss when the activated-hazard variable is removed;
  \item a history-conditioned A* reference planner with an exact small-hazard return-connectivity oracle, together with a same-oracle state-only fixed-field control that isolates representation from estimator quality;
  \item a frozen 96-scenario generated challenge suite in which every feasible route activates hazards, plus controlled, heterogeneous, and hand-authored geometric evaluations and a hard safe-return objective-sensitivity study;
  \item a structural critical-cut approximation with both a measured scaling regime and an adversarial multi-cell-cut counterexample that exposes its failure boundary.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\subsection{Safe return and contingency feasibility}
Moldovan and Abbeel make returnability central to safe exploration in non-ergodic MDPs \cite{moldovan2012safe}. SafeMDP likewise combines safe reachability with returnability so exploration does not enter states with no safe way out \cite{turchetta2016safemdp}. Zhang and Guo enforce high-probability return to home states while learning an uncertain MDP online \cite{zhang2022online}, and Guo \emph{et al.} jointly synthesize outbound and safe-return behavior under probabilistic temporal tasks \cite{guo2023safereturn}. Safe exploration methods also evaluate multi-step reachability and returnability probabilities before visiting uncertain regions \cite{stephens2024safeexploration}. Backup Plan Constrained MPC optimizes primary and alternative mission feasibility \cite{tao2024backup}. Contingency-MPPI embeds contingency generation inside nominal planning, and SCRAMPPI uses Hamilton--Jacobi reachability to certify that a safe-set trajectory exists from every state on the nominal plan \cite{jung2025contingency,srirangam2026scramppi}. These methods are broader and, in several cases, provide stronger safety or dynamics guarantees than our grid abstraction. Our distinction is not the safe-return objective itself, but the representation of future return-connectivity when the set of active environmental hazards is caused by executed robot transitions.

\subsection{History-dependent motion risk}
Xiao \emph{et al.} explicitly reject a purely state-dependent risk model, classify action- and traverse-dependent risks, and plan with directional history information \cite{xiao2020risk}. Their framework is close to ours in the reason memory is required: two arrivals at a geometric location need not have the same path risk. Our problem variable differs. We condition on a set of action-triggered \emph{future environmental closures} and evaluate the induced probability that the current state remains connected to a safe set. Thus the object being propagated is future return-connectivity under a changed environmental hazard state, rather than accumulated locomotion risk alone. We treat the 2020 result as a direct novelty constraint, not as a weak baseline.

\subsection{Traversal-dependent topology}
Self-deleting graphs explicitly model traversal-dependent structure: processing a vertex can delete edges from the residual graph \cite{carmesin2023selfdeleting}. Shortest pathfinding on this model has been studied algorithmically and shown to be computationally hard in several restricted graph classes \cite{dvorak2025selfdeleting}. In robotics, Frenkel \emph{et al.} use self-deleting graphs to model coverage with self-induced obstacles \cite{frenkel2026selfinduced}. These works are direct novelty constraints because they already establish that the robot's previous traversal can alter future graph feasibility. DynNav differs in the modeled random variable and evaluation target: executing a trigger activates, but does not immediately realize, a stochastic future closure; the quantity of interest is the conditional probability that a safe set remains reachable. We therefore treat deterministic self-deletion as neighboring prior art rather than claiming the general history--topology connection as new.

\subsection{Predictive commitment and dynamic topology}
Classical Canadian-Traveller formulations study navigation with blocked or uncertain edges that are revealed online \cite{papadimitriou1991shortest}; this is an important uncertain-topology precedent, but the edge realization is exogenous rather than activated by the traveller's executed transitions.
RCSP studies commands that are locally safe but commit a robot to a bottleneck that moving obstacles may soon close \cite{han2026rcsp}. Dynamic door-closing work has used POMDP belief-tree reasoning for self-protective manipulation behavior \cite{liu2021door}, while nondeterministic planning has long represented exogenous events and robust plans \cite{perret1996events}. These works motivate proactive reasoning about future changes; they do not by themselves establish that our action-triggered return-topology abstraction is novel.

\subsection{Endogenous uncertainty and degradation}
Optimization under endogenous uncertainty explicitly models probability distributions that depend on decisions \cite{doan2022endogenous}. Baldes \emph{et al.} model utilization-driven, state-action-dependent actuator failures and their long-term effect on future control capability \cite{baldes2024fallible}. Consequently, neither ``history matters'' nor ``actions can reduce future capabilities'' is a contribution here. We instead focus on a falsifiable robotics question: whether history contains return-connectivity information that is discarded when environmental closure probabilities are represented only as state-only marginals.

\section{Problem Formulation}
Let $G=(V,E)$ be a traversable grid graph, $x_t\in V$ the robot position, and $\Ssafe\subseteq V$ a safe set. A topology hazard
\begin{equation}
h_i=(e_i,c_i,p_i)
\end{equation}
contains a directed trigger transition $e_i\in E$, a currently traversable closure cell $c_i\in V$, and a future closure probability $p_i\in[0,1]$. Traversing $e_i$ activates hazard $i$. For path history $H_t=(x_0,\ldots,x_t)$, define
\begin{equation}
\Aset(H_t)=\{i\mid e_i\text{ occurs in }H_t\}.
\end{equation}
The augmented planning state is $z_t=(x_t,\Aset(H_t))$. Figure~\ref{fig:history-gap} illustrates the minimal same-endpoint distinction.

For a fixed augmented state, each distinct active closure cell denotes one latent Bernoulli closure event with a fixed probability, and these events are independent across cells. Multiple triggers mapped to the same cell activate that same event; they do not create independent repeated trials. The model has no within-mission closure realization or observation update: the future closure sample is drawn for the return-connectivity evaluation, and the robot cell is conditioned traversable at that instant. Under these assumptions, the active trigger set determines the active-cell probability map, so $(x,\Aset)$ is a sufficient Markov state for the modeled return query. It need not be minimal: trigger identifiers that induce identical active-cell maps can be merged. Correlated closures, changing probabilities, partial observations of realized events, or time-dependent closures require additional state (for example, a joint belief or event-time state), and are outside this model. Let $C$ denote the random set of realized closures. We define exact safe-return reliability
\begin{equation}
\Rret(x,\Aset)=
\Pr\!\left[\exists\text{ path in }G\setminus C\text{ from }x\text{ to }\Ssafe\right].
\label{eq:return}
\end{equation}
For small active sets, Eq.~\eqref{eq:return} is computed exactly by enumerating closure realizations and testing connectivity.

\subsection{Why geometric state alone is insufficient}
A state-only marginal model maps position $x$ and a fixed marginal hazard field to a single return estimate $\widetilde R(x)$. It therefore cannot assign distinct values to two histories that end at the same $x$.

\textbf{Lemma 1 (history information gap).}
There exists a grid, safe set, trigger hazard, and histories $H^a,H^b$ with the same endpoint $x$ such that
\begin{equation}
\Rret(x,\Aset(H^a))\neq\Rret(x,\Aset(H^b)),
\end{equation}
while any deterministic state-only marginal model returns the same $\widetilde R(x)$ for both histories.

\emph{Proof.} Let one history traverse a trigger that activates closure of the unique bridge to the safe set with probability $p>0$, and let the other history reach the same endpoint without traversing that trigger. Then the triggered history has return reliability $1-p$ and the non-triggered history has reliability $1$. Since both end at the same $x$, a state-only function of $x$ and fixed marginals must return one common value. \hfill$\square$

\textbf{Corollary 1 (endpoint-only approximation lower bound).}
Let two executable histories terminate at the same geometric state $x$ with true return reliabilities $R_1$ and $R_2$. Any deterministic endpoint-only scalar estimator $g(x)$ satisfies
\begin{equation}
\max\{|g(x)-R_1|,|g(x)-R_2|\}\geq \frac{|R_1-R_2|}{2}.
\label{eq:alias-lower-bound}
\end{equation}
\emph{Proof.} The triangle inequality gives $|R_1-R_2|\leq |R_1-g(x)|+|g(x)-R_2|$. If both errors were smaller than half the separation, this inequality would be violated. \hfill$\square$

For the bridge construction above, $|R_1-R_2|=p$, so every deterministic endpoint-only estimate has worst-history error at least $p/2$. This is an elementary representation lemma, not a claim of new state-abstraction theory: state aggregation and abstraction in MDPs have long studied the consequences of discarding decision-relevant state information \cite{li2006stateabstraction}. The bound only formalizes the specific aliasing induced by removing the activated-hazard variable in our model.

\section{Planning Methods}
\subsection{History-conditioned A* with exact return oracle}
Search is performed over augmented states $(x,\Aset)$. For transition $z\rightarrow z'$, the soft-history objective uses
\begin{equation}
c(z,z')=1+\lambda\left(1-\Rret(z')\right),
\label{eq:cost}
\end{equation}
with nonnegative recoverability weight $\lambda$. Publication-facing runs use heuristic weight 1.0, so Manhattan distance scaled by the positive unit step cost is admissible and consistent for this additive objective; no optimality claim is made for configurations using heuristic weight greater than 1. The online return-oracle calls used to select the route are included in measured planning latency; post-hoc diagnostics are excluded.

\subsection{State-only fixed-field baselines}
The state-only controls receive the same closure cells and conditional closure probabilities $p_i$ but drop the activation variable. They therefore apply one fixed hazard field at a geometric state regardless of which triggers were actually executed. We retain the original single-best-return-path estimator as an engineering baseline and add a stronger \emph{state-only exact} baseline that uses the same exact connectivity oracle and the same additive fragility objective as the history-conditioned planner. The latter is the primary representation ablation: estimator and objective are matched, while conditioning on activated hazards is removed.

\subsection{Hard safe-return baseline}
A strong constraint baseline accepts a successor only when
\begin{equation}
\Rret(z')\geq\tau,
\end{equation}
for a threshold $\tau$. This abstracts safe-return and contingency methods that enforce backup feasibility rather than optimize a soft penalty.

\subsection{Critical-cut approximation}
Exact enumeration scales exponentially in the number of active hazards. We therefore evaluate a structural approximation that removes each active hazard cell individually and identifies those whose single removal disconnects the robot from $\Ssafe$. Multiplying the survival probabilities of these individually critical cells gives
\begin{equation}
R_{\mathrm{cut}}=\prod_{i\in K}(1-p_i).
\end{equation}
Because multi-cell cutsets may disconnect the graph even when no individual cell is critical, $R_{\mathrm{cut}}$ is an upper bound in general. This sits within classical stochastic-network reliability, where exact connectivity computation is hard in general and cut-based bounds are well established \cite{provan1983reliability,ball1983networkreliability}. We therefore treat the approximation as scalability infrastructure, not as a novel graph-theoretic contribution.

\section{Experimental Design}
All stochastic comparisons use common random numbers: a seed generates one latent draw for every declared closure event, and a planner realizes only the events it activated. This preserves paired event-index outcomes even when planners activate different subsets. The empirical failure metric is post-closure inability to reach the safe set in the resulting grid; we refer to this as post-closure return infeasibility. Failed trials remain in binary denominators; undefined continuous metrics are counted explicitly rather than silently removed.

The evaluation is organized around four research questions.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item[\textbf{RQ1}] \emph{Representation:} can identical geometric endpoints have different return reliability, and what error is unavoidable when those histories are collapsed to one endpoint-only estimate?
  \item[\textbf{RQ2}] \emph{Planning consequence:} does conditioning on activated hazards change route choice and post-closure return infeasibility when compared with both approximate and exact state-only fixed-field controls, including worlds where every feasible route activates hazards?
  \item[\textbf{RQ3}] \emph{Objective sensitivity:} is the observed benefit evidence for the history-conditioned representation, or for superiority of the particular soft recoverability cost over hard safe-return constraints?
  \item[\textbf{RQ4}] \emph{Computation:} what is the cost of exact return-connectivity reasoning, when does a critical-cut approximation help, and where does that approximation fail?
\end{enumerate}

The analytic route-switch experiment supports RQ2 by checking a pre-derived decision boundary rather than only reporting sampled outcomes. The same-oracle state-only exact control isolates the representation effect in RQ1--RQ2, while the soft-versus-hard sweep and joint-cut counterexample are explicit falsification tests for RQ3--RQ4.

The development sweeps use homogeneous closure probabilities. Before inspecting outcomes, a separate repeated-module evaluation family was frozen with heterogeneous per-trigger probabilities and unseen module counts: six modules with probabilities $(.15,.25,.35,.55,.75,.85)$, seven modules with $(.80,.20,.70,.30,.60,.40,.90)$, and eight modules with $(.10,.90,.20,.80,.35,.65,.45,.75)$. Each scenario uses 500 paired execution seeds. This tests probability-pattern and horizon transfer within the same procedural topology family.

A second held-out family was frozen with three different hand-built grid topologies: a fork, an L-shaped room, and a two-trigger chamber. Starts, goals, safe sets, trigger transitions, closure cells, and probabilities were fixed before the 500-seed outcomes were inspected. Following reviewer audit, a separate generated challenge suite was frozen before execution with seed 20260925: 24 scenarios contain one to four serial route-choice modules, and every start-to-goal path must activate at least one hazard per module. Direct and detour hazard probabilities are drawn from a committed discrete set, detour depth and direction vary by module, and each scenario uses 500 paired execution seeds. Thus no planner can obtain zero modeled risk merely by selecting a trigger-free path. After those worlds were frozen, a separate objective-sensitivity protocol was committed before its outcomes were inspected: soft weights $\lambda\in\{0,1,2,4,8,16\}$ and hard thresholds $\tau\in\{.50,.70,.80,.90,.95,.99\}$, again with 500 common-random-number seeds per configuration. Planning infeasibility is reported separately from empirical post-closure return infeasibility.

\section{Results}
\subsection{Same state, different history}
The counterfactual benchmark sweeps $p\in\{0.1,0.3,0.5,0.7,0.9\}$. The safe history has exact return reliability $1$, while the trigger-taking history has reliability $1-p$. A state-only marginal model assigns $1-p$ to both because it cannot condition on whether the trigger was traversed. Across the five cases, the mean history separation is $0.5$ and the maximum is $0.9$; the triggered-history invariant is matched with zero numerical error. By Eq.~\eqref{eq:alias-lower-bound}, a separation of $p$ implies that every deterministic endpoint-only scalar estimate incurs worst-history error of at least $p/2$. The particular marginal baseline used here assigns $1-p$ to both histories, giving zero error on the triggered history but error $p$ on the trigger-free history. Thus the experiment illustrates a representation lower bound rather than only the behavior of one chosen baseline.

\subsection{Analytic phase boundary}
In a controlled world with a direct path of length $4$ and a trigger-avoiding detour of length $4+2d$, exactly one direct transition incurs fragility $\lambda p$. The predicted route switch is therefore
\begin{equation}
\lambda p>2d.
\end{equation}
Across 75 combinations of detour depth, closure probability, and weight, the exact history-aware planner matches the analytic route choice in all 75 cases. The shortest-path baseline never detours.

\subsection{Soft versus hard objective sensitivity}
The predeclared parameter sweep provides an important negative scope result. In the fork, soft $\lambda=0$ and $1$ select the risky length-6 route with failure rate 0.810, while $\lambda\ge2$ selects the length-8 zero-hazard route with zero observed post-closure return-infeasible outcomes. In the L-room and chamber, $\lambda=0$ retains the risky route, while every tested $\lambda\ge1$ selects a zero-hazard route. By contrast, every frozen hard threshold $\tau\in\{.50,.70,.80,.90,.95,.99\}$ selects a zero-hazard route with zero observed post-closure return-infeasible outcomes in all three worlds. All tested configurations are planner-feasible.

This result prevents an objective-level overclaim. The geometric held-out experiments support the value of representing activated hazard history; they do not show that the soft cumulative penalty dominates a hard safe-return constraint. On these worlds the hard baseline is at least as effective and has lower retained single-run planning latency. The two objectives answer different operating questions, and neither is universally preferred by the present evidence.

\subsection{Approximation and scaling}
On a series-critical family, the critical-cut estimate is exact by construction. We replace the earlier single-shot timing with 100 retained repetitions after 10 warm-up calls. \subsection{Joint-cut adversarial case}
The critical-cut approximation is exact on the series family because each relevant closure cell is individually critical. We therefore construct a different topology with two parallel return corridors. Neither closure cell alone disconnects the safe set, but closing both does. The lattice has two equal-length goal routes (length 6), so route choice isolates the recoverability estimate rather than a distance tradeoff.

Across all nine combinations $p\in\{0.2,0.5,0.8\}$ and $\lambda\in\{2,4,8\}$, exact history-aware planning selects the route that activates no hazards and retains exact return reliability 1. The cut planner selects an equal-length route that activates both hazards in all nine cases. Because neither cell is individually critical, it reports $R_{\mathrm{cut}}=1$. Exact post-hoc evaluation of that same route is instead $1-p^2$: 0.96, 0.75, and 0.36 for the three probabilities. Thus the artifact contains nine of nine optimistic cut estimates and a route-history disagreement rate of 1.0. This is an explicit counterexample to using individually-critical cuts as a general return-reliability surrogate.

\section{Discussion}
The experiments support a narrow claim. When actions activate future topology hazards, path history can contain information about safe-return connectivity that is absent from a state-only marginal hazard map. This is not equivalent to claiming that history-aware planning is universally safer, that soft recoverability costs dominate hard contingency constraints, or that the proposed model subsumes general dynamic navigation.

The same-oracle state-only null result is more informative than comparison only against shortest path. The exact state-only control and the history-conditioned planner use the same connectivity oracle and additive fragility objective; they differ in whether return probability is conditioned on executed trigger activations. The fixed-field control reproduces shortest behavior in the controlled, heterogeneous, geometric, and 96-scenario generated challenge evaluations. In the unavoidable suite the history-conditioned planner cannot escape stochastic commitments: it instead changes which hazards become active, improving 36 scenarios while remaining nonworse across all 96. This strengthens the representation interpretation without implying arbitrary-map or physical-environment generalization.

The hard safe-return results sharpen this interpretation. A fixed $\tau=0.9$ differs from the soft objective in the eight-module heterogeneous case, but the complete frozen threshold sweep on the three geometric worlds selects the same zero-hazard behavior for every tested threshold. Thus the strongest conclusion concerns the state representation---activated environmental hazard history---rather than superiority of a particular soft objective. The soft formulation remains useful when one wants an explicit path-length/fragility tradeoff, whereas hard constraints encode an admissibility requirement.

The exact oracle is useful for small controlled worlds and for validating approximations, but its exponential closure enumeration is not a scalable navigation solution. The critical-cut approximation removes most of this cost in series-articulation families. Its joint-cut failure is therefore an important design boundary: fast structural approximations require multi-cell cut reasoning, scenario sampling, or another estimator when redundant return paths can fail jointly.

The closest history-dependent risk work also narrows the interpretation. Xiao \emph{et al.} already establish that robot risk can depend on action and traversal history and that planning may need a history-sensitive state representation \cite{xiao2020risk}. Our evidence does not supersede that result. It isolates a different state variable: which future environmental topology changes have been activated and what that implies for safe-set connectivity.

Self-deleting graph work provides an equally important topology-side boundary \cite{carmesin2023selfdeleting,dvorak2025selfdeleting,frenkel2026selfinduced}. In those formulations, traversal deterministically changes the residual graph. DynNav should therefore be read as a stochastic return-connectivity specialization, not as the first history-dependent topology model: trigger execution changes the distribution over future residual graphs, and the planner reasons about the probability of retaining a return path.

\section{Threats to Validity}
The homogeneous and heterogeneous series families contain critical return bridges and were designed to expose the mechanism, so a trigger-avoiding policy has a structural advantage when closure probabilities are appreciable. The three geometric held-out worlds reduce dependence on that single repeated topology but are still hand-built to contain action-triggered return-critical closures. The expanded frozen generated challenge reduces this specific concern because every feasible route activates hazards and evaluates 96 generated scenarios, but its serial choice-module generator is still a structured synthetic family rather than a distribution of natural environments. Consequently, the large observed risk differences are mechanism-demonstration effect sizes for these constructed worlds; they should not be interpreted as estimates of the effect size expected in natural or deployed environments. The state-only baselines intentionally apply the same fixed closure field regardless of executed triggers; their failure demonstrates information loss under that modeling choice, not weakness of every possible Markov representation. The exact fixed-field variant uses the same connectivity oracle as the history-conditioned planner specifically to remove estimator quality as a confound. A sufficiently rich Markov state could include the activated-hazard set, which is exactly our augmented state.

Parameter selection remains a concern for any weighted objective. The principal soft-planner execution results use $\lambda=8$, but the predeclared geometric sweep spans $\lambda\in\{0,1,2,4,8,16\}$ and hard thresholds from 0.50 to 0.99. That sweep shows both where the soft route switches and where hard constraints match the zero-hazard behavior; it therefore reduces, but does not eliminate, parameter-selection concerns. Monte Carlo uncertainty remains in empirical failure rates. Paired risk differences and bootstrap intervals are the primary inferential summaries because seeds are shared across planners; exact McNemar tests are retained only as secondary diagnostics.

\section{Limitations}
The current study uses discrete grid connectivity, known trigger locations, known closure probabilities, and mostly independent Bernoulli future closures. A scenario-based oracle demonstrates that equal marginals can be optimistic under correlated closures, but correlated uncertainty is not yet integrated into the online planner. The stochastic execution experiments sample topology events after commitment; they do not model continuous robot dynamics, perception delay, collision, or contact.

The repository contains a ROS~2 Jazzy/Nav2 C++ implementation of the history-conditioned planner and a frozen Gazebo action-trigger protocol. The C++ plugin retains activated hazards from externally observed executed transitions rather than from planned paths, and its search and plugin contracts are covered by software tests. Strict retained Gazebo mechanism probes contain eight paired repetitions per planner (24 total trials per run); all trials are valid and all outbound navigations succeed. The paired latent closure realizes in seven of eight repetitions. In one retained run, DynNavShortest executes the configured trigger in 8/8 trials and DynNavHistory in 7/8; in a later exact-head rerun both execute it in 8/8, with the realized closure applied in 7/8 for both. Neither run provides a stable comparative execution effect, and the exact-head rerun records no irreversible failures for either DynNav condition. We therefore treat Gazebo as end-to-end integration and measurement evidence only and make no simulation-efficacy, hardware, collision-avoidance, kinodynamic-safety, or formal runtime-guarantee claim. The three geometric held-out worlds and the 96-scenario generated unavoidable-hazard suite provide broader mechanism replication, but neither establishes arbitrary-map or real-world geometric generalization.

Finally, literature search cannot prove novelty. Safe exploration and returnability, safe-return constraints, contingency planning, predictive commitment, history-dependent risk, self-deleting graphs, self-induced obstacles, endogenous uncertainty, dynamic doors, and utilization-driven degradation all have substantial prior art \cite{moldovan2012safe,zhang2022online,guo2023safereturn,xiao2020risk,carmesin2023selfdeleting,dvorak2025selfdeleting,frenkel2026selfinduced,jung2025contingency,srirangam2026scramppi,han2026rcsp,baldes2024fallible,liu2021door,doan2022endogenous}. The defensible contribution is the explicit stochastic trigger--activation--realization model for environmental return-connectivity, the endpoint-aliasing lower bound, and its controlled evaluation, subject to continued prior-art review.

\section{Reproducibility}
The implementation records raw per-trial CSV files and machine-readable JSON summaries for every benchmark family. Focused continuous integration runs regression tests before executing the benchmark suite, then uploads all raw artifacts under the exact commit SHA. Planning-time instrumentation excludes post-hoc diagnostic profiling while retaining oracle work used online for route selection. Stochastic comparisons use fixed paired seeds and event-index common random numbers. The heterogeneous, geometric held-out, generated unavoidable-hazard, and soft-versus-hard protocols were frozen before their respective outcomes were inspected. A machine-readable paper evidence manifest records the evidence-producing workflow runs, artifact identities and digests, metric values, and claim scope.

\section{Conclusion}
We studied navigation in which the robot's own transition history activates stochastic future closures that can destroy return connectivity. In this setting, the same geometric cell can correspond to different recoverability because the environment's future topology depends on how the robot arrived. An exact state-only fixed-field control uses the same connectivity oracle and objective yet cannot condition on this activation variable. In a frozen 96-scenario suite where every feasible route activates hazards, history-conditioned planning improves 36 scenarios, is nonworse in all 96, and reduces mean post-closure return-infeasibility from 0.744 to 0.666 at a modest path-length cost. A frozen objective sweep also shows that hard safe-return constraints can match the soft objective in trigger-avoidable worlds, so the evidence supports the history-conditioned representation rather than universal superiority of a particular cost. A critical-cut approximation reduces computation in series-critical cases but fails on joint cutsets. These results establish a reproducible mechanism-level case for history-conditioned safe-return reasoning while delimiting the additional evidence required for broader robotics claims.
